South Korea's nuclear taboo has never rested on a principled renunciation of nuclear weapons, as in Japan, but on a "managed silence" propped up by the ROK-US Atomic Energy Agreement, the international non-proliferation regime, and the American nuclear umbrella. Tracing this ambivalence from Park Chung Hee's clandestine weapons program through the 2004 IAEA revelation to the diverging Yoon Suk Yeol and Lee Jae-myung governments, this paper argues that the taboo is now eroding under the combined pressure of North Korea's advancing capabilities, rising public support for nuclear armament, the mainstreaming of tactical redeployment and nuclear-sharing proposals, and the paradoxical institutional legitimacy lent by the 2023 Washington Declaration and Nuclear Consultative Group. This erosion is a nonlinear process driven by the interaction of threat perception, alliance credibility, and domestic political contestation. Examining six drivers — public opinion, conservative politics, media agenda-setting, the North Korean threat, ambiguous signals from Washington, and lessons from the Ukraine war — the paper explains why the taboo has held so far, identifies the conditions under which it could cross an irreversible threshold, and outlines pathways for sustaining it through public education, civil society, and political engagement.
